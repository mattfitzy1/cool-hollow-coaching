# A Pricing System for Trade Businesses

Working note, July 5, 2026. Standalone solution to the pricing pain Mike named: trade businesses show almost no understanding of cost, pricing, and measurement, and could double or triple profit with structured pricing. This note solves that as its own problem.

---

## The reframe

Trade businesses don't have a pricing problem. They have a costing problem that shows up as a pricing problem. The system fixes knowledge first, then price, then holds it in place with measurement. Three layers.

## Layer 1: Know the true cost

The core error: pricing labor at the wage. A $30/hour tech costs $42-50/hour paid once burden is added (payroll taxes, workers comp, benefits, PTO, truck, tools, insurance), and is only billable 55-70% of paid hours. At 60% billable, that tech costs $70-80 per billable hour.

Four numbers, buildable in a week:
1. **Burdened labor cost** = total annual cost of a tech / paid hours.
2. **Billable efficiency** = billable hours / paid hours (track two normal weeks).
3. **Overhead per billable hour** = all other annual spend / total company billable hours.
4. **Break-even rate** = (burdened labor + overhead) per billable hour. Every job below this loses money.

## Layer 2: Build the price

- **The rate:** break-even / (1 - target net margin). Written down, used on every quote.
- **Materials:** cost x (1 + waste factor) x sliding markup (high % small-ticket, lower % big-ticket).
- **Flat-rate over hourly** wherever possible: customer buys an outcome, fast skilled crews earn more per hour, no driveway rate argument.
- **Three-option quotes (good / better / best):** cheapest revenue lever in trades; middle option anchors; average ticket typically rises 15-25%.
- **Structure, not exceptions:** service-call minimum, after-hours/urgency premiums, lower rate reserved for contracted maintenance customers only.

## Layer 3: The governor (measurement)

- **Job-level scoreboard, weekly:** quoted vs actual hours and margin on every job. Without it the system decays in ~90 days.
- **Close rate as the pricing thermostat:** winning >75-80% of quotes = underpriced. Healthy band for a quality shop: 55-70%. Rule is mechanical: close rate above band, raise 5% on new quotes, watch 30 days, repeat. Pricing becomes a dial, not a courage question.

## The fear-killing math

Tolerable volume loss from a price increase = increase / (increase + contribution margin).
- 30% CM shop raising 10% can lose **25% of jobs and earn the same money**, on less work.
- Same shop cutting 10% needs **+50% volume** to stand still.
Underpricing is the expensive strategy, not the safe one.

## Stress tests

| Scenario | Result |
|---|---|
| $2M HVAC, 5% net, 8 techs at $30/hr, 60% billable | True cost ~$75/billable hr + ~$30-35 overhead = ~$108 break-even vs $115 charged. Move to $135 flat-rate + 3-option quotes: even at -10% volume, profit ~$100K to ~$250K. Reproduces the "double or triple" claim from first principles. |
| Project-based trade (glazing) | Same system, bid form: burdened hours + materials with waste + overhead + margin + install-risk contingency. Quoted-vs-actual review matters even more. |
| Genuine low-bid commodity market | System can't force margin the market won't give; it names the floor so losing bids get walked from. Honest limit: fixing a commodity position takes differentiation, a separate problem. |
| Messy books | Build the cost model bottom-up from payroll records + 90 days of bank statements instead of the P&L. Less precise, close enough. |
| "My market won't take it" | Cheap reversible test: new rate on new quotes only, contracts grandfathered with 60 days notice, measure close rate 30 days. Holds = assumption wrong. Craters = real market position learned for the cost of a month, dial back one notch. |

## Rollout order for a real shop

1. Week 1: build the four cost numbers (bottom-up from payroll + bank statements if books are messy).
2. Week 2: write the rate card, build the three-option quote template, set the service-call minimum.
3. Week 3: new rates live on new quotes only; existing contracts get 60 days notice.
4. Weeks 3-7: weekly scoreboard (quoted vs actual, close rate). Adjust by the thermostat rule.

## One-sentence version

Burden the labor, allocate the overhead, price off a written rate with three options, and let close rate, not courage, decide when prices move.
