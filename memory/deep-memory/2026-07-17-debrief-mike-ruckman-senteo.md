### ACE offering model (Attractors, Core, Expanders)
- Attractors: solutions to immediate, often event-based problems (reporting issue, production launch, opening a store). Cheap or low-friction entries: masterminds, workshops, podcasts, networking events, possibly the course.
- Core relationship anchors: things that improve quality of life on an ongoing basis. For Cool Hollow: (1) the community and (2) ongoing retainers. These are the relationship heart of the model.
- Expanders: project-based work tied to goals and aspirations (e.g. design a career development model for a client's employees). Delivered in-house, by partners, or blended (80/20, 50/50, 20/80). Partner-delivered pieces can still be monetized.
- The ladder: attractor gets them in, community holds the relationship, dialogue surfaces aspirations, expanders monetize the deep work.
- Mike: "I really honestly think that like three to five times more revenue could be just from the existing clients... if it was priced properly."

### Retainer redesign (Mike's workstream)
- Principle: "The retainer should be a nice, ongoing taste of everything with no deep work happening. And any deep work should be an expander product."
- Hands-on implementation work is not covered by $5,000 a month; it gets an assigned team and a separate fee.
- "The things that they're getting asked to do for free today, they'll get paid for."
- Supermarket analogy for clients: we teach you a new recipe every month and you cook for yourself; if you want us to come cook for a month, that is a separate engagement.

### Pricing psychology and the challenge model
- Blue-collar owners have no experience with consultants and fear being taken advantage of; there is already pushback writing checks for $3K to $5K a month.
- Pay-what-you-want course idea: "it costs $10,000, but at the end you decide how much value you got... We challenge you to not want to pay us at the end of this course."
- Mike used this on real projects: free discovery up front, "we challenge you to not want to engage with us after this." Builds trust fast. Clients who refused to pay were clients not worth keeping.
- Matt asked directly whether that is the right way to get the first five clients of a new offering: yes.
- War stories: a bank client that hesitated, then paid; the project created ~$350M in value over 9 months and the bank became the largest privately owned bank in Russia. Later an $8M, 3-year exclusive engagement where the client covered all team salaries.
- Exclusivity as a premium lever: Ukraine, ~$2M/year from one bank purely for exclusivity; Georgia, $400K/year not to work with other banks in the country.
- Mark's personal time as premium tier (Matt's idea, Mike agreed): if Mark wants to wind down in ~2 years, price him at $20K/month, "if you don't want me, go to my team, they're cheaper." Hobby clients.
- Productizing and practice building: offerings must be designed so average people can deliver them at low cost. You cannot staff everything with mega experts, and you cannot cheap-sell expert time.

### Community design (Matt's workstream)
- Paid, not free. Matt: "even if it's a hundred bucks a month, people will be way more likely to be active on the community because they paid for it." Mosey principle: if it's scalable, it's cheap; if it's not scalable, make it expensive.
- Tier ideas: $20 / $100 / $500. The $500 level could include advisory, e.g. a monthly live one-to-many call.
- Structure: topic matrix using Cool Hollow's four domains (People, Strategy, Operations, Financial) crossed with industry groups (e.g. finance in HVAC). Start with one group; expand as engagement grows.
- Member profiles, searchable (find peers: "CEO, HVAC, similar size"), plus direct messaging. Peer-to-peer advice is a core draw.
- Discussion groups actively seeded: at Senteo, Mike had employees responsible for keeping content flowing (articles, book discussions) when members went quiet.
- Book reviews and reading lists worked well in the Senteo community (that content later moved to the public site).
- Content/learning database and courses (LMS) live inside the community.
- No feed. "People are going to go to Facebook or LinkedIn for a feed." Direct messaging and discussion groups instead.

### Community as an acquisition channel
- Current channels (networking events, boardroom, LAN advisory, the Cool Hollow mastermind, referrals) have high acquisition costs. Matt estimated roughly $200K/year across travel and mastermind fees.
- A $50 to $200/month community becomes a low-cost acquisition layer and a marketable audience that feeds the mastermind and retainers. Additive, not a replacement for existing channels.
- Matt's observation: the last mastermind was existing clients and friends, not strangers. It gets marketed but does not funnel properly.
- Mark has proven the one-to-many motion: his first Cool Hollow Solutions clients came from free-information webinars; his first webinar drew ~100 people; he brought on a banker, a lawyer, etc. He has done the community-shaped thing before, just never monetized it.

### Frameworks Mike taught
- Experiential innovation: product/channel innovation is rational ("what I get for the price I pay") and at the radical end produces disruptive technologies. Experiential innovation is emotional ("what it means to me") and at the radical end produces disruptive relationships (Harley-Davidson: a brand people tattoo on their bodies). Combined, balanced offerings produce disruptive offerings (iPhone: roughly 13-20% global unit share but ~70% of industry profit).
- Relationship dynamics (Mike to send, may only exist in Russian): five components that keep people engaged in a community long term: relevance; trust and security; belonging; status and reputation; growth and transformation.
- Mike's article on AI (sent during call): use AI to make humans more efficient and effective, not to replace them. Matt's four-things frame from the conversation: information, accountability, implementation, relationship; AI only covers information.
